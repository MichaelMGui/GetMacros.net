Dialysis Nutrition: Protein, Fluid and Mineral Basics

Dialysis changes nutrition because treatment removes wastes, fluid and some nutrients. The plan differs by hemodialysis versus peritoneal dialysis, treatment frequency, urine output, laboratories and appetite.

Never copy another patient’s limits. Dry weight, potassium, phosphorus, protein and fluid prescriptions are individualized and can change after hospitalization or modality changes.

Protein commonly needs more attention

Dialysis removes amino acids and protein, especially with peritoneal dialysis. Adequate protein supports muscle, immunity and healing. The quantity and sources should come from the renal dietitian.

Sodium affects thirst and fluid gain

Lower-sodium choices can make fluid management easier. Check packaged foods, restaurant meals, sauces and processed meat. Potassium salt substitute is not a safe automatic replacement.

Fluid needs follow urine output and treatment

Someone still producing urine may have a different allowance from someone producing very little. Home or more frequent dialysis can also change limits. Count ice, soup and other fluids according to the team’s instructions.

Potassium and phosphorus need laboratory context

Dialysis removes some potassium but not always enough; phosphorus is also difficult to control. Food changes, dialysis prescription and medicines work together. Do not skip dialysis or alter binders to compensate for meals.

Peritoneal dialysis adds glucose exposure

Dialysate contains glucose, some of which is absorbed. This can affect appetite, glucose management and energy intake. People with diabetes need coordinated guidance rather than cutting food unpredictably.

Watch for malnutrition

Poor appetite, nausea, taste changes, infection and repeated hospital stays can cause loss of weight and muscle. Report reduced intake early. Oral nutrition products vary in potassium, phosphorus, sodium and protein and should be selected with the renal team.

Prepare for treatment days

Ask what to eat before, during and after treatment, how to handle low blood pressure or nausea, and where to keep safe snacks. A practical plan should fit transportation, work and fatigue.
