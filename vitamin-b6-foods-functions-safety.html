Vitamin B6: food sources, functions, and why supplement dose matters

Vitamin B6 supports amino-acid metabolism, neurotransmitter production, hemoglobin formation, and immune function. Unlike food intake, chronic high-dose supplementation can injure nerves.

Original editorial guide · Updated August 13, 2026

What vitamin B6 does

Vitamin B6 refers to several related compounds; pyridoxal 5'-phosphate is the main active coenzyme form. B6-dependent enzymes participate in protein metabolism, neurotransmitter production, glycogen use, hemoglobin formation, and immune function.

Adult intake references

The U.S. recommendation is 1.3 mg per day for adults ages 19–50. For adults 51 and older it is 1.7 mg for men and 1.5 mg for women. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise the values to 1.9 and 2.0 mg, respectively.

These are milligram amounts. Supplements sold with tens or hundreds of milligrams are not simply “a little extra.”

Food sources

Poultry, fish, organ meats, potatoes and other starchy vegetables, chickpeas, bananas, and fortified cereals provide vitamin B6. A varied pattern usually supplies B6 across several meals rather than from one dominant food.

Deficiency context

Isolated B6 deficiency is uncommon and often appears with deficiencies of other B vitamins. Kidney disease, malabsorption, alcohol dependence, and some autoimmune conditions or medicines can affect status. Signs can include anemia, skin or mouth changes, depression, confusion, and weakened immune function, but none is diagnostic on its own.

The supplement safety issue

Long-term use of high-dose vitamin B6 supplements can cause severe sensory nerve damage, with numbness, pain, or trouble controlling movement. Problems have been reported from supplements, not ordinary food intake.

Different authorities have reviewed upper limits as evidence evolves. The practical point is stable: check the total B6 dose across multivitamins, B-complex products, energy products, and single-nutrient supplements, and do not use high doses chronically without clinical direction.

Some medicines can interact with or affect B6, including certain antiseizure and tuberculosis drugs. A pharmacist can review the complete list.

Check the supplement stack, not just one bottle

Vitamin B6 can appear in a multivitamin, B-complex, magnesium blend, pre-workout, energy drink, sleep product, and single-nutrient tablet at the same time. Add the labeled doses across products before deciding an intake is modest. The name may appear as pyridoxine hydrochloride or pyridoxal-5-phosphate; a different form does not make a very high chronic dose automatically safe.

A food-first pattern can include chickpeas or another legume, potatoes, poultry or fish, bananas, and fortified grains across different meals. The goal is not to force every source into one day but to create several reliable routes over time.

If numbness, burning, balance changes, or altered sensation develops while using supplements, stop assuming the product is benign and seek clinical advice. Bring photographs or the containers so the clinician can see dose, form, frequency, and all overlapping ingredients.
