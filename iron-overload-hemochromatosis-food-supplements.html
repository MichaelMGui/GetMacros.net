Iron Overload and Hemochromatosis: Food and Supplements

Iron overload can damage the liver, heart, pancreas and joints. Hereditary hemochromatosis is one cause, but high ferritin can also reflect inflammation, liver disease, infection or metabolic conditions.

Do not self-treat a high ferritin by extreme food restriction or blood donation. Diagnosis uses transferrin saturation, genetics and clinical context, and treatment must be supervised.

Ferritin is not a pure iron gauge

Ferritin rises during inflammation and liver injury. One high value does not prove hemochromatosis, while family history and persistently high transferrin saturation may raise suspicion.

Stop unnecessary iron only with guidance

People with confirmed overload are generally told to avoid iron supplements and high-dose vitamin C supplements unless prescribed. Check multivitamins, sports products and fortified powders.

Food restriction has limits

Treatment often relies on therapeutic phlebotomy, not an iron-free diet. Meat portions and fortified products may be discussed, but adequate protein and overall nutrition remain important.

Raw shellfish can be dangerous

Iron overload and liver disease increase vulnerability to severe infections from certain bacteria in raw shellfish. Avoid raw or undercooked shellfish and follow food-safety guidance.

Alcohol can compound liver risk

Alcohol advice depends on liver injury and treatment. Discuss use openly. A person with significant liver disease may be advised to abstain.

Blood donation is not interchangeable with treatment

Therapeutic phlebotomy follows schedules and laboratory monitoring. Community donation eligibility and intervals serve different purposes; follow the treating clinician and blood center.

Family implications matter

Hereditary conditions may prompt family counseling or testing. Do not infer a relative’s status from symptoms or diet.
