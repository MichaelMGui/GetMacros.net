Liver-health nutrition

MASLD and Fatty Liver: A Food-Pattern Guide

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously commonly called NAFLD, involves excess liver fat in a metabolic context. It is often silent and cannot be diagnosed from symptoms or body size alone.

Clinical assessment matters: Liver enzymes can be normal despite disease. Diagnosis and fibrosis risk may involve history, blood tests, imaging and specialist evaluation.

Use an eating pattern, not a liver cleanse

A Mediterranean-style pattern—vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, nuts, fish and unsaturated oils—can support cardiometabolic health. It does not need imported ingredients or a rigid menu.

Start with sugary drinks and refined snacks

Soda, sweet tea, energy drinks and large sweet coffees can add rapidly absorbed sugar. Replace a frequent drink, then improve meals by adding vegetables, beans or whole grains rather than banning every carbohydrate.

Fat quality matters

Choose more unsaturated fat from olive or canola oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and fish, and less saturated fat from processed meat, butter and some high-fat dairy. “Low fat” desserts are not automatically liver healthy.

Movement helps beyond weight

Aerobic and resistance activity can improve metabolic health even when scale weight changes little. Build from current ability and medical limits. Exercise is treatment support, not punishment.

Weight loss is not the only conversation

When clinically appropriate, sustained weight loss can reduce liver fat and inflammation. Aggressive dieting can worsen muscle loss, eating distress and regain. Discuss medicines or surgery when relevant instead of framing them as failure.

Manage the related risks

Diabetes, blood pressure, cholesterol and sleep apnea often matter to long-term outcomes. Keep prescribed medicines and screening plans; food does not replace them.

Alcohol needs individual advice

Alcohol can add liver risk, especially with fibrosis. Be honest about amount and pattern and ask whether abstinence is recommended. If dependence is possible, abrupt stopping can be dangerous without medical support.
