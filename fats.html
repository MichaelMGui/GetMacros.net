Fat · 9 calories per gram

Fat: hormones, vitamins & cell structure

Dietary fat has a bad reputation it doesn't deserve. It builds every cell membrane in your body, is the raw material for steroid hormones, and is required to absorb four essential vitamins. Cutting it too low has real consequences.

What fat actually does

Fat is the most calorie-dense macronutrient at 9 calories per gram — more than double protein or carbs — which makes it an efficient way to store energy. But energy storage is only one of its jobs. Dietary fat is a core structural component of every cell membrane in your body, provides cushioning and insulation for organs, and helps regulate inflammation and blood clotting.[1]

Fat also comes in different forms with different effects on health: saturated fat (meat, butter, coconut oil), unsaturated fat (olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty fish), and industrially-produced trans fat, which raises LDL cholesterol and is best minimized.[2]

Fat and hormone production

Several major hormones — including testosterone, estrogen, and cortisol — are steroid hormones synthesized from cholesterol, which your body gets partly from food and partly makes on its own using dietary fat as a building block. Because of this, chronically very low fat intake has been studied as a factor in reduced hormone output: a systematic review and meta-analysis of intervention studies found that men following low-fat diets (roughly 20% of calories from fat) had measurably lower testosterone than men on higher-fat diets (roughly 40% of calories from fat).[3]

This doesn't mean "more fat is always better" — it means going too low, for too long, can work against you if hormone health, muscle building, or reproductive health are a priority.

Absorbing fat-soluble vitamins

Vitamins A, D, E, and K are fat-soluble — they dissolve in fat rather than water, and dietary fat is required to absorb them from food in your gut and transport them through your bloodstream. Eating vegetables with zero added fat can mean missing out on much of their vitamin content.[4]

Essential fatty acids

Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids are "essential" — your body cannot make them, so they must come from food (fatty fish, walnuts, flaxseed, vegetable oils). True essential fatty acid deficiency is rare in people eating a varied diet, but has been documented in patients on fat-free intravenous nutrition, underlining that these fats are not optional.[5]

What happens if you're deficient

  • Hormonal imbalance — reduced production of testosterone, estrogen, and other steroid hormones
  • Poor absorption of vitamins A, D, E, and K — even if you eat enough of these vitamins, low fat intake can blunt how much you actually absorb
  • Dry, irritated skin — linked to insufficient essential fatty acid intake
  • Menstrual irregularities — very low fat and low overall energy intake are linked with disrupted menstrual cycles
  • Poor satiety — fat slows digestion and helps you feel full; very low-fat diets are often harder to stick to

How much fat do you need?

The Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR) for fat is 20–35% of total calories for adults.[6] Below 20%, meeting essential fatty acid and vitamin needs gets difficult; consistently going well above 35% typically displaces protein and carbohydrate intake.
