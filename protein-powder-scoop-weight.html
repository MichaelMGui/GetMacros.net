Food labels

Use the powder’s serving weight, not just the scoop count

A scoop is a product-specific serving measure. Use the label’s gram weight to calculate partial servings and include the liquid in your shake separately.

In this guide

By GetMacros · Published October 3, 2026

Check how many grams the label calls one serving. A scoop is a measuring aid for that product, not a universal amount of protein. “30 g powder” and “30 g protein” are different statements.

FDA’s serving-size guidance explains that nutrient values belong to the stated serving. For powder, keep that serving weight visible when calculating a smaller or larger amount.

An original scoop example

Suppose a hypothetical product lists one 32 g serving of powder as 120 calories and 24 g protein. You weigh the powder itself, excluding the scoop or mixing cup.

Powder usedServing multiplierCaloriesProtein
16 g0.56012 g
24 g0.759018 g
32 g112024 g
40 g1.2515030 g

For the 24 g portion, use 24 ÷ 32 × 24 = 18 g protein. Multiplying 24 g powder by a claim of “24 g protein per serving” without dividing by the 32 g serving would mix two different quantities.

What if the scoop is heaped?

If you need to know the actual amount, measure the powder’s weight. A scoop filled above its intended level can contain more powder than the label’s serving. Switching flavors or products is another reason to check the label again rather than carrying over the old scoop value.

The shake includes more than powder

Water does not turn a 24 g powder portion into a larger powder serving. If you add milk, a plant drink, yogurt or fruit, calculate those ingredients separately and add their nutrients to the shake. Check whether the manufacturer’s prepared-shake column already includes a specified liquid.

This page explains the arithmetic; it does not recommend a supplement or a protein dose. Read the protein-target guide for the broader question, or compare protein cost using the actual servings per package.
