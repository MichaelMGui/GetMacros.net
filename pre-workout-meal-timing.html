What to eat before a workout

A pre-workout meal should support the session without becoming the session’s main challenge.

By the GetMacros.net editorial team · Reviewed August 13, 2026 · General education, not individualized sports or medical advice

Quick answer: Plan food before training using time available, session demands, digestion, and personal tolerance rather than one universal rule.

Timing changes the meal

A meal three or four hours before training can be larger and more mixed. Food within an hour is usually smaller and easier to digest. The closer exercise gets, the more useful familiar, lower-fiber, lower-fat carbohydrate choices may become for people prone to gastrointestinal discomfort.

Match the session

A short easy workout may require no special meal. Long endurance training, high-volume lifting, or multiple daily sessions can make pre-exercise carbohydrate more relevant. Protein in a prior meal can contribute amino acids, but there is no need to force a shake immediately before every workout.

Test in training

Tolerance varies. Some people train well after breakfast; others prefer more time. Practice the exact food and timing before competition. New supplements, unusually high fiber, large portions, and heavy fried meals add avoidable uncertainty.

How long before, and how much

The further out the meal, the larger and more mixed it can be. Close to training, the constraint is digestion rather than nutrition — blood is being diverted to muscle, and a heavy meal sitting in the stomach is the usual cause of cramping and nausea.

Time beforeSizeComposition
3–4 hoursFull meal, 500–700 kcalProtein, carbohydrate, some fat and fiber
2–3 hoursModerate, 300–500 kcalProtein and carbohydrate, lower fat
1–2 hoursSmall, 200–300 kcalMostly carbohydrate, some protein, minimal fat and fiber
Under 1 hourSnack, 100–200 kcalFast carbohydrate only — banana, toast, sports drink

Fat and fiber are what get cut as the window narrows. Both slow gastric emptying, which is useful at breakfast and unhelpful thirty minutes before a hard session.

Training fasted, and when it matters

For short or moderate sessions, training fasted is fine and for some people more comfortable. The claim that it burns more fat is technically true and practically irrelevant: fasted training shifts fuel use toward fat during the session, but total daily energy balance decides fat loss, and the difference washes out over the day.

Where fasted training does cost something is in performance on longer or harder sessions, and in muscle preservation when the last protein feeding was many hours earlier. If you train early and fasted, the case for protein soon afterwards is stronger than it is for someone who ate two hours before.
