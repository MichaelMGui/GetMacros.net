Label math is an estimate, even when the division is exact

Your shelf

Understand rounded calories and nutrient values, avoid false precision when scaling, and distinguish a labeled zero from missing restaurant data.

In this guide
What a neat answer can hideBefore blaming rounding, check the bigger errorsZero and unknown are different

By GetMacros · Published October 3, 2026

Food labels report rounded values. Multiplying a serving by 1.5 can produce a decimal, but that decimal is the result of your calculation—not a new measurement of the food.

FDA’s rounding guidance in Appendix H shows that different nutrients use different rounding rules. A small discrepancy between calories and a calculation from displayed macro grams is not, by itself, evidence that a label is wrong.

What a neat answer can hide

Consider this invented arithmetic example. An underlying amount of 12.4 g is displayed as 12 g after rounding to the nearest whole gram. For three servings, multiplying the displayed figure gives 36 g; multiplying the illustrative underlying figure gives 37.2 g.

Starting numberThree-serving calculationMeaning
Displayed 12 g12 × 3 = 36 gThe total you can calculate from the label.
Illustrative underlying 12.4 g12.4 × 3 = 37.2 gShows how rounding can accumulate; not a value you can infer for a real food.

Do not “correct” a real label by adding 0.4 g. You generally do not know its underlying unrounded amount. Use the published values and acknowledge their precision.

Before blaming rounding, check the bigger errors

  1. Are you reading the serving column or the whole-package column?
  2. Does your portion use grams, milliliters or a count of pieces?
  3. Does the prepared column already include milk or oil?
  4. Have you counted every component only once?
  5. Are both values from the same product, country and version of the label?

A double-counted drink or wrong serving multiplier can matter much more than a small decimal discrepancy.

Zero and unknown are different

A label displaying zero is a published claim subject to that label’s rules. A blank restaurant nutrient field means we do not have a supported value. It must not become zero merely so a calculator can add it.

For comparisons, retain the published calories and macro values independently. Avoid reconstructing an unknown nutrient by subtracting rounded calorie estimates. Read how missing values affect meal comparisons.
