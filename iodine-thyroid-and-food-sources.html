Iodine: thyroid function, iodized salt, and food sources

Iodine is required to make thyroid hormones, which regulate many aspects of metabolism and development.

Home › Micronutrient library › Iodine: thyroid function, iodized salt, and food sources

By the GetMacros.net editorial team · Reviewed August 13, 2026 · Educational content, not medical advice

Quick answer: Understand iodine’s role in thyroid hormone production, where it appears in food, and why both too little and too much can cause problems.

What it does

The thyroid incorporates iodine into its hormones. Requirements rise during pregnancy and lactation because iodine supports fetal and infant development. Deficiency and excess can both disrupt thyroid function.

Food sources and absorption

Iodized salt, seafood, dairy products, eggs, and some fortified foods can contribute. Specialty salts are not automatically iodized. Seaweed content varies widely, so it is an unreliable way to dose iodine precisely.

Safety and individual context

People with thyroid disease, those avoiding dairy and seafood, pregnant people, and anyone considering kelp or high-dose iodine supplements should seek individualized advice. More is not safer.

Primary reference

NIH Office of Dietary Supplements consumer fact sheet

How to use this information

Reference values describe population needs and are not a diagnosis. Food patterns, health conditions, pregnancy, laboratory results, and medications can change what is appropriate. Avoid high-dose supplementation unless there is a clear reason and qualified guidance.
