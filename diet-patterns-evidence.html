Diet names are a starting point.

Compare what an eating pattern includes, what its evidence can tell you, and what a food log cannot.

The ingredient, portion and overall pattern matter more than a label. This is an evidence-reading guide, not a prescription or a ranking.

Animal-based and lower-carbohydrate labels

“Animal-based” has no single standardized recipe or nutrient target. Ask what it includes before treating it as a comparable intervention. “Lower carbohydrate” also requires a stated amount and context; it should not automatically be read as ketogenic or as a disease-treatment recommendation.

For either label, examine the foods actually eaten, the comparison diet, study duration and outcomes. An isolated nutrient total cannot establish overall adequacy or a health effect.

How to read a diet claim

  1. Identify the study design: anecdote, survey, observational study, controlled trial or clinical guidance.
  2. Check who participated and how they were selected.
  3. Separate reported satisfaction from measured clinical outcomes.
  4. Check duration, missing data, funding and whether the result applies to your situation.

Use the notebook without turning it into a grade

Compare actual portions and recipes in the food library. Missing micronutrient fields do not show a deficiency, and one logged day does not define a dietary pattern.
