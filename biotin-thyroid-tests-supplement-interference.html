Biotin and Thyroid Tests: Supplement Interference

Biotin can interfere with laboratory assay technology, making some thyroid results look abnormal even when thyroid function has not changed. The problem is the test—not necessarily the gland.

Do not delay urgent testing. Tell the clinician and laboratory what you took and when. Follow their instructions rather than choosing your own washout period.

Biotin appears in many products

Hair, skin and nail supplements can contain doses far above dietary needs. Multivitamins, prenatal products, energy formulas and separate B-complex products may overlap.

Interference can point in misleading directions

Depending on assay design, results may mimic hyperthyroidism or obscure another problem. Biotin can also affect non-thyroid tests, including some cardiac assays.

Read the Supplement Facts panel

Find biotin in micrograms or milligrams and check serving size. “Beauty blend” marketing may hide the importance of disclosure even when the amount is listed.

Ask before stopping

Professional guidance often recommends withholding high-dose biotin for a period before testing, but the interval depends on dose, kidney function and assay. Use the laboratory or clinician’s exact instructions.

Food biotin is not the main concern

Normal foods contain modest amounts and do not usually create the same exposure as high-dose supplements. There is no need for a biotin-free diet before routine labs.

Repeat testing may be necessary

If results conflict with symptoms or prior history, clinicians may repeat tests after an appropriate interval or use a different method. Do not change thyroid medication based on a surprising result without review.

Keep a product photo

Photograph the front label and Supplement Facts. Exact dose and last use are more useful than saying “I take vitamins.”
