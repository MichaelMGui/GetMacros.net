A dry-milk serving can represent the powder needed for a cup of prepared milk, but the answer comes from the label’s serving description. Water changes how much you pour and how much each sip contains. It does not contribute the protein listed for the powder.

Which portion applies to a glass or a recipe?
SituationWhat to count
One portion of powder mixed and consumed in fullOne dry serving, or the matching prepared serving
Two dry servings go into a jug and you drink a quarterHalf of one dry serving, assuming the mix is evenly distributed
Powder added straight to batterThe weight of the dry ingredient for the batch, then the share of the finished batch eaten

Measure the finished batch

Suppose the powder used for a pitcher lists 24 g protein in total, and the finished mix measures 720 mL. A 240 mL glass is one third of the whole batch, or 8 g protein. If you instead add enough water to bring the same powder to 960 mL, a 240 mL glass now gives a quarter of the total, or 6 g. Those figures are an example, not a product label.

Pick one entry in the diary

USDA’s household sheet gives an example of preparing a cup from nonfat dry milk powder. Use the instructions for the particular package you bought. Some labels give a dry serving in grams and some also list a prepared amount. Use the dry amount or the corresponding prepared entry in your food diary. If you used milk instead of water, include its nutrients in the recipe. If a label already gives figures for that preparation, check which ingredients are included before adding anything again.
