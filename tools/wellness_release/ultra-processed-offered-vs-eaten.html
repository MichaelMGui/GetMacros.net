The NIH trial found that 20 adults ate about 508 more kcal per day, on average, during two weeks of the experimental ultra-processed diet than during two weeks of the comparison diet. Participants could eat as much or as little as they wanted. The reported difference describes how much they chose from two sets of complete menus in a controlled setting.

Three different questions about ultra-processed food
QuestionEvidence that answers it
What were they offered?The menus were designed to match the offered calories and several nutrients, including macronutrients, sugar, fiber and sodium.
What did they eat?Researchers measured intake from what each participant actually ate under each assigned condition.
What is this grocery food?The NOVA authors describe processing and formulation clues, which you check against the ingredient list and the product itself.

The difference matters for a shopping decision

The team also observed differences beyond the processing label, including higher energy density among the non-beverage foods in the ultra-processed condition. This experiment cannot isolate a rule for an individual ingredient, predict a shopper’s specific intake or assign 508 kcal to opening a single package.

For a shelf comparison, use the portion you would actually eat and compare its label to the alternative. A processing classification answers a separate question about how the product was made. Use the comparison tool to keep those serving sizes visible.
