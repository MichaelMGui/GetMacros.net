Meal studies can show a short-term change in a cortisol measurement. They do not show that a food “detoxes” cortisol. Cortisol has normal functions and varies over the day. Removing a toxin is a different claim from measuring a hormone after a meal.

Two experiments that answer different diet and cortisol questions
ExperimentWhat was measuredWhat the result can support
Liquid meals with carbohydrate, protein or fat versus a low-calorie controlPlasma cortisol after the mealAfter each meal, plasma cortisol increased about 90 nmol/L relative to control in this experiment in men
Two four-week meal schedules with earlier or later high-carbohydrate mealsSaliva sampled every four hours over 24 hours after each interventionThe 29 men showed a daily cortisol rhythm, and rhythm patterns were similar between the two schedules
Claim that a drink “clears” chronically elevated cortisolWould require a defined group, a measured abnormality and an appropriate controlled outcomeNeither of the meal experiments tests this claim

Ask what ‘lower’ is being compared with

A drink at noon followed by a lower value at bedtime cannot separate a drink effect from the usual daily decline. A same-time control, repeated measurements and enough follow-up would address different parts of that problem. Check whether the source compared like with like before treating a change as evidence of an effect.

A suspected hormone disorder calls for a clinical assessment. NIDDK describes several tests used in that setting, including late-night saliva and 24-hour urine measurements. A meal experiment cannot substitute for those tests. See also what a photo can tell you about a cortisol claim.
