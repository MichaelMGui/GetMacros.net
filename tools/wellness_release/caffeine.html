Two controlled trials found that a large caffeine dose could disrupt sleep hours before bedtime. The studies tested specific doses and times. The result does not supply a single coffee cutoff for every person or establish the caffeine in your particular drink.

What the researchers changed

Two controlled caffeine studies. Times are hours before habitual bedtime.
StudyDose and timingResult in the study
Drake et al., 2013400 mg at bedtime or 3 or 6 hours earlierThe researchers found sleep disruption at all three times compared with placebo.
Gardiner et al., 2025100 mg or 400 mg at 4, 8 or 12 hours earlierThe 400 mg dose affected sleep outcomes. The 100 mg conditions did not detect a significant effect in this sample of 23 men.

Use the dose and the clock together

For a hypothetical midnight bedtime, a 6 p.m. drink is six hours before bed, while an 8 p.m. drink is four hours before bed. A person looking only at the time would miss another difference if one drink supplied 100 mg and the other 400 mg. Use a label or the publisher’s caffeine information for that specific drink. Calories and serving volume cannot substitute for a caffeine value.

Why the smaller-dose result has limits

The Gardiner study used a randomized crossover design, meaning each member of the sample took part in different conditions. That helps compare each person with themselves. The sample still consisted of 23 men who habitually consumed moderate amounts of caffeine. The study’s four-hour result at 100 mg cannot establish what happens for all people or for a dose right before bed. An average finding also has limited power to describe an individual.

For a practical reading of your own routine, record the latest drink, its published amount if available, and the intended bedtime. The examples here illustrate the study timings. Use clinical guidance for persistent sleep problems.
