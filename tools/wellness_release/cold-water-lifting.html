Two resistance training studies found smaller gains in at least one measure of muscle size when participants used cold-water immersion repeatedly after training. Their strength results differed. That difference matters when a headline claims all training gains will go down after an ice bath.

Roberts et al. (2015)Muscle mass increased more with active recovery over the 12-week strength training comparisonStrength increased more with active recovery
Fyfe et al. (2019)The cold-water group showed smaller growth in muscle fiber sizeThe study did not find reduced strength gains in the cold-water group

Ask what the word “gains” is measuring

Muscle fiber size is assessed in a sample of tissue. A strength test measures performance on a specific task. A group could change on one measure without showing the same change on the other. In the Roberts paper, there was also a separate single-session experiment with muscle biopsies at several time points. A chemical signal measured hours after training is a separate piece of evidence from how muscle and strength changed after 12 weeks.

Check the frequency in the claim

Try substituting “after each training session in the study” for the phrase “after a workout.” That small edit makes clear what the comparison covers. It does not tell us what happens after an occasional cold exposure. These trials address specific strength training and recovery programs, not every kind of exercise or every person.
