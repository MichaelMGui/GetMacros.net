In two experiments, raters gave photos taken after sleep loss different average scores from photos of the same people after normal sleep. They tended to see the sleep-restricted faces as more tired. The experiments tell us how observers rated particular pictures. They did not measure whether someone’s skin changed or whether a particular person had slept enough.

Separate the person photographed from the person doing the rating

The larger number in each study refers to observers, not additional participants who underwent sleep loss. Photographing each participant in both conditions reduces the problem of comparing people whose faces differ for many reasons. It still leaves a limited group of people whose images were tested. The two studies used different sleep restrictions, so their scores cannot be read as a single estimate of how much an hour of sleep changes appearance.

Read “perceived health” as an impression

In the 2017 study, ratings of sleepiness differed while perceived trustworthiness did not. A phrase such as “looked less healthy” refers to a rating category. It is separate from blood tests, a skin examination or evidence of lasting changes. If a post points to a pair of photos, check whether the same lighting, expression and distance were used before attributing any difference to sleep.
