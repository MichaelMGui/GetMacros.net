In a small head-to-head study, more magnesium appeared in urine after participants took magnesium citrate than after magnesium oxide under the study conditions. That result supports a difference in absorption under those conditions. It does not identify which form improves sleep, symptoms or any other outcome the study did not measure.

Compare the serving before comparing forms

Imagine one U.S. Supplement Facts panel lists 100 mg magnesium as citrate per two capsules and another lists 100 mg magnesium as oxide per one capsule. Each panel would be declaring the elemental amount for its serving. The hypothetical citrate label supplies 50 mg per capsule and the oxide label supplies 100 mg per capsule. The amount provided and the fraction absorbed are different variables. These invented amounts illustrate the math.

What the urine finding measures

Lindberg and colleagues measured solubility and urinary magnesium after matched amounts of magnesium in the two forms. More urinary magnesium during the measurement windows was consistent with higher bioavailability of citrate, as summarized by NIH. Urinary excretion alone does not establish how much an individual retained or whether a clinical outcome improved. An ad that cites the absorption paper for a sleep claim would need a study that measured sleep.
