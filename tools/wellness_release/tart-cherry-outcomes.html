One tart cherry juice study found a difference in reported pain after a relay. In a separate small marathon trial, the clearer result was recovery of isometric strength, a measure of force without joint movement. The studies addressed different outcomes, so a broad “helps recovery” headline hides part of the evidence.

What the studies actually measured
EvidenceOutcomeReported result
Kuehl and colleagues, running relaySelf-reported post-run muscle painThe reported increase was smaller in the juice group.
Howatson and colleagues, 20 recreational marathon runnersStrength recovery and a set of additional markersIsometric strength recovered faster in the juice group. The other muscle damage markers did not differ significantly.
2026 synthesis, 19 trialsSeparate pooled outcomes for strength, jumping, soreness and biomarkersThe main strength estimates favored juice, with substantial variation across studies. No clear pooled effect on soreness was found.

Read the effect together with the measure

A person can report less pain and still produce the same amount of force. A study can also show the reverse. Ask whether the tested exercise resembles yours, whether the outcome was rated or directly measured, and when researchers checked it.

The synthesis rated its certainty from very low to moderate across different outcomes. These findings do not transfer automatically to every cherry-flavored product. See the guide to soreness and muscle recovery for another comparison of these measures.
