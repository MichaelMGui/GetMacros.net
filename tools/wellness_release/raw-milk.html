Pasteurization reduces the risk from harmful microbes in milk. The FDA says it does not meaningfully change milk’s nutritional value, and Health Canada likewise describes the nutritional composition as similar. “Raw” on a milk label describes heat treatment. It does not tell you the fat content, fortification or nutrients in the portion you drink.

How to make a fair milk comparison

Suppose one carton describes unpasteurized whole milk and another describes pasteurized low-fat milk. If their fat values differ, you have changed both heat treatment and fat content. To compare the roles of the two factors, first match the fat level and serving size. Check whether either product is fortified before interpreting a difference in listed vitamins. Keep the labels and the food-safety question separate.

Four checks when a milk claim compares two cartons
ClaimWhat to check
“More protein”Match the serving volume and compare grams of protein.
“More vitamins”Name the vitamin and check fortification on each product.
“Better for lactose intolerance”Heat treatment and lactose removal are separate processes. FDA reports the same lactose concentration in raw and pasteurized milk.
“Safer because the farm is clean”Check the safety claim against official guidance. Clean handling alone does not assure that raw milk is free of pathogens.

Nutrition numbers cannot check for bacteria

Even two cartons with identical nutrition figures can have different food-safety risks. Health Canada identifies harmful microorganisms including Salmonella, Campylobacter and E. coli as possible hazards in raw milk. The practical takeaway from the public-health guidance is to choose pasteurized milk. For protein, calcium or calories, compare the exact product labels as a separate step.

This comparison covers drinking milk. Regulations for raw milk cheeses and other products can differ by jurisdiction. Follow current local food-safety guidance for those products.
