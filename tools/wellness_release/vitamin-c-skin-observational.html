A food reference can tell you how much vitamin C a portion contains. The NIH lists 70 mg in one medium orange. In a separate study, higher reported vitamin C intake was associated with lower odds of a wrinkled appearance among 4,025 women aged 40–74. That study used an observational survey and assessed diet with a 24-hour recall.

Three questions that require different evidence
QuestionEvidence to useWhat to keep in the result
How much vitamin C is in the lunch?Food measured at the amount and preparation servedOne medium reference orange provides about 70 mg.
What did the survey observe?Reported diet and a skin assessment across participantsAn association in the surveyed population after statistical adjustments.
Did changing one food alter a wrinkle measurement?A comparison that assigns the change and follows a defined skin outcomeDifferences between groups over time, plus measurement uncertainty.

Adjusting for measured factors helps, but the diet recall still captures a limited window and the study did not assign oranges or isolate a single food. A label or composition table is useful in the first row. It cannot substitute for the third row.

Adopt the actual outcome into the wording when evaluating claims. “Associated with wrinkled appearance in a survey” gives more context than a promise about what will happen after eating a food. For another source of differences in skin studies, see how researchers account for sun exposure.
