Temperature alone leaves out humidity, workload and duration. Two settings with different temperatures can impose comparable or different heat strain depending on the rest of the conditions. An occupational study that contrasts hot, dry work with warm, humid work asks a much narrower question than how much any person should drink outside.

Before applying a fluid-balance claim to a different setting
Find in the sourceReason the detail matters
Temperature and humidityHumidity affects how readily sweat can evaporate. An indoor dry-heat trial and humid outdoor practice are distinct settings.
Workload and breaksThe activity itself produces heat, and rest changes the exposure over time.
Fluid access and accountingCheck whether people could drink freely or followed a schedule, and how the researchers measured fluid balance.

Match the setting before comparing the numbers

A hot-versus-cool soccer study also compares training intensities. If a post quotes one sweat-loss number from it, find the corresponding intensity and environment first. A result from elite players cannot stand in for a worker, a recreational walker or a short indoor session. The CDC guidance adds the practical context that outdoor exertion and individual susceptibility affect heat risk.
