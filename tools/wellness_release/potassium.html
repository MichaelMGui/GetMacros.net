A medium banana provides about 422 mg of potassium in the NIH table. A medium baked potato, using the flesh-only entry, provides 610 mg. A cup of cooked lentils supplies 731 mg. But half a cup of those lentils supplies about 366 mg, below the banana for that smaller serving.

Take the part you ate

That potato figure uses flesh only. If you eat the skin, use a reference that includes it when one is available. “Medium” is also a reference size. A weighed slice from a much larger potato is better compared with the matching per-100-g entry. Weighing the banana peel would include weight outside the usual edible portion.

Keep foods and salts separate in the comparison

These figures describe food portions. NIH distinguishes potassium naturally found in foods from potassium chloride used in some salt substitutes. A spoonful of a salt substitute is a different product with its own label, not a substitute record for the potato or lentils. Product labels and individual health needs are separate from this comparison.

Use one basis at a time

For an order or plate, compare the portions you expect to eat. For a density comparison, use corresponding reference records on an equal-weight basis. Changing the denominator can change the order of the rows, as the lentil example shows. The U.S. reference amounts above are a sample, so they support this comparison without establishing the highest-potassium food overall.
