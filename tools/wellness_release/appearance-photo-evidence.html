Two photos can make the same face look different if the camera moved, even when the person’s face did not change. To judge a diet or skincare claim that relies on before-and-after photos, start by checking whether the pictures were taken under comparable conditions.

Camera distance can change proportions in the image

Ward and colleagues built a geometric model of how facial proportions appear at different camera distances. A separate study measured facial landmarks in photos taken at different settings with a smartphone and a full-frame camera. Both address what appears in a photograph. Neither establishes that a person’s facial anatomy changed.

A checklist for evaluating a fictional two-photo claim
What changed between photos?Why it matters for the comparison
Close selfie vs. portrait taken farther awayCamera position can change apparent proportions even when the pictures are cropped to the same size.
Side lighting vs. direct lightingThe photo can show different shadows, which changes how an edge or contour appears.
Neutral face vs. a different expression or poseThe landmarks being compared may no longer be displayed in comparable positions.
Several routine changes over the same monthEven two technically matched photos cannot isolate the effect of a single food or product.

Match the evidence to the promise

Say a TikTok compares a close phone photo with a headshot after a month of changing its author’s diet. Matching the viewing size cannot undo a change in perspective. Ask for repeated images with comparable camera position, lighting and expression. Then ask what else changed over the month. Better photographs improve comparison, while a study with controls is needed to make a stronger claim about cause.
