Researchers can use how much light skin reflects at several wavelengths to help distinguish pigments. A randomized six-week smoothie study in Malaysian Chinese university students found a pattern consistent with carotenoids. A simple before-and-after photo gives much less information.

How to read the study’s two kinds of signals
MeasurementWhat it tells a readerWhat it cannot establish
Color coordinatesThe drink group’s instrument-measured yellowness increased and redness increased by a smaller amount.The color coordinate alone does not identify a pigment.
Reflectance by wavelengthReduced reflectance in roughly the 450–510 nm range was consistent with absorption by more than one carotenoid.It does not give an exact amount of food consumed by an individual.
Ordinary phone photoA visual record influenced by lighting, camera settings and angle.Those changes can mimic a shift in skin color.

Read the comparison rather than judging someone’s photo

The smoothie trial assigned a drink or water. Another study followed reported fruit and vegetable intake over six weeks and found a related reflectance pattern. Together they show why the optical method matters, without making skin tone a health grade or proving the same effect for all people. A pigmentation result also provides no substitute for evidence about UV protection.
