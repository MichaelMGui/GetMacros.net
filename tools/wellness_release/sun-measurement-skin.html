A skin study can use questionnaires, diaries or UV badges to estimate sun exposure. That choice matters when one group spends more time outdoors than another. A nutrition effect is easier to interpret when exposure is recorded or the study separately assigns an intervention related to sun exposure.

Time outside and UV exposure are different inputs

The four-day diary aligned more closely with the badge readings than the retrospective questionnaire did in that study. A higher correlation does not turn a diary into a dose meter. Two people can report an hour outside and receive different exposure. Measurement itself can affect behaviour. A separate Danish experiment found that recording exposure could increase participants’ attention to sun-related behaviour.

In a skin-outcome study, also check whether the investigators assigned a sunscreen intervention separately from a supplement, as one Australian trial did. That design helps interpret the assigned effects. It answers a different question from how accurately people remember outdoor time. See how the graders in that trial assessed skin changes.
