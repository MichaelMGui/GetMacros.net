Half a cup of raw diced portabella mushrooms is listed separately at 0.1 micrograms, with no UV treatment identified in that row.

Read the package you bought

Look for three details together: the mushroom variety, any stated UV exposure and the amount of vitamin D per labeled serving. Suppose a U.S. label lists 8 mcg per 85 g. If your recipe uses 170 g and splits it evenly across four plates, each plate gets 42.5 g of the raw ingredient. That represents 4 mcg per plate from the label before any change during preparation. The example uses a fictional package, so use your own label when logging real food.

Why the comparison is not a UV experiment

The reference rows differ in variety and cut, and the half-cup portions may weigh different amounts. Their difference illustrates why picking the right row matters. Estimating the effect of UV exposure itself would require comparable samples of the same mushroom measured before or after a defined treatment. NIH explains that UV exposure can increase vitamin D2 in mushrooms. The table does not give a predictable amount for home storage or preparation.

For a packaged mushroom with no matching vitamin D figure, keep that nutrient unknown in your food diary instead of borrowing the UV-exposed value. The reference food explorer supports comparing closely matched foods by weight.
