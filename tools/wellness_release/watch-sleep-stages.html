A sleep tracker estimates deep sleep. Whether it does a good job depends on the device and on which measurement you compare with a reference. A device can perform reasonably on the question “asleep or awake?” while still assigning individual stages differently from a sleep-lab recording.

Three separate claims about a sleep tracker
ClaimWhat the comparison needs
It measures total sleep timeCompare the overall estimated number of minutes with a reference recording
It can tell when you were awakeCompare individual intervals classified as asleep or awake
It gives the amount of deep sleepCheck agreement with a stage-by-stage reference, including errors between light and deep sleep

Why the same total can hide a different night

Imagine a device and a sleep lab both report seven hours asleep. If the device calls one 30-minute interval deep sleep and the lab calls it light sleep, the total remains the same. That example is invented. The distinction explains why total duration agreement cannot validate the tracker’s deep-sleep minutes on its own.

Read the device study that matches the claim

Chinoy and colleagues compared seven consumer devices with polysomnography in a sleep lab. Their results varied by device and by whether the outcome was sleep or wake detection or stage assignment. A later study compared six specific wrist-worn models with polysomnography. Those results apply to tested versions and settings. They cannot certify an unnamed watch’s current algorithm, and a consumer score cannot establish a diagnosis. See how regularity adds a different measurement for another example.
