In the NIH reference table, a cup of raw spinach lists 145 micrograms of vitamin K, a cup of raw kale lists 113 and half a cup of cooked chopped broccoli lists 110. All three figures describe portions ready to eat. If two cups of raw spinach go into a salad, that is a different comparison from half a cup of a cooked side.

Try the salad and side as separate portions

If one plate includes a half-cup of raw baby spinach and half a cup of cooked broccoli, estimating the spinach from the reference row gives 72.5 mcg. Add the broccoli for a combined estimate of about 183 mcg, using the source’s rounded numbers. How tightly leaves are packed affects the amount of spinach a measuring cup holds, so a measured weight and a matched weight-based food record give a clearer estimate.

What would a cooking comparison require?

Collards and broccoli have the same listed volume in the table, but they are different plants. Raw spinach and cooked broccoli differ in both species and preparation. To measure how much of the nutrient survived cooking, start with a batch of the same vegetable, record the edible weight before and after, and compare the total amount in the batch. Comparing equal cups can pick up a change in volume alongside any change in nutrient content.

The listed plant foods are primarily a comparison of phylloquinone, or vitamin K1. NIH marks foods such as natto separately when its figure refers to a form of vitamin K2. If you take warfarin, ask your prescriber how to manage changes in vitamin K intake. The portions here are for reading the food table.
