A small study linked changes in reported fruit and vegetable intake with some changes in measured skin reflectance over six weeks. The researchers also ran a separate experiment on how observers judged changes in images. The two parts measured different things. Neither makes any skin color a target for health.

Read the exact result behind the headline

Whitehead and colleagues measured diet and color in 35 people at the start and at two follow-up visits. One facial yellowness association was near the study’s statistical cutoff, with a reported probability value of 0.051. Looking at a different site or outcome can give a different result. One association cannot prove that a particular food caused a change in a specific person.

Keep measured color separate from an impression

An instrument measurement records a property of the light reflected from the skin. An observer’s answer reflects how they judged a shown image in that task. If a food ad combines the two, ask which outcome its cited paper actually tested. A photo comparison needs its own controls for lighting, camera setup and expression.
