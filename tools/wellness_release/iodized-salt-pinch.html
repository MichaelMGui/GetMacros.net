NIH lists 78 micrograms of iodine in a quarter teaspoon of iodized table salt. One measured sixteenth of a teaspoon would supply about 20 micrograms if the same salt is used throughout. The word “pinch” has no fixed amount, and a pinch of noniodized sea salt is a different ingredient.

Count the recipe and the plate separately

If a hypothetical soup uses a quarter teaspoon of the reference iodized salt and is divided into four equal bowls, each bowl gets an estimated 19.5 mcg from the salt before any changes in preparation. The recipe could contain additional iodine from other ingredients. If some broth is left behind, an even-bowl calculation no longer describes exactly what you ate.

Check the front and ingredient list

“Sea salt” describes a type of salt. NIH notes that specialty salts are usually not iodized, but the package can say whether iodine was added. A label listing sodium alone does not establish iodine content. If the package gives the iodine content of your product, use that figure rather than a reference average. If you know only that a restaurant dish includes salt, leave the iodine amount unknown. The nutrient explorer demonstrates why checking the named food and portion matters.
