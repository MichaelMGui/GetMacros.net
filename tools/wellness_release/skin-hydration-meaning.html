When a skin study reports “hydration,” look for the instrument, the part of the body tested and the time of the reading. An electrical reading on the surface of the forearm, a mechanical test of how skin returns to shape, and an image scored by a rater answer different questions.

What to check when a study reports a change in skin hydration
MeasurementWhat it captures in the studyWhat you still need to know
Skin capacitanceAn electrical property used as an indirect estimate of hydration in the outer skinWhere the probe was placed and when the reading was taken
Skin deformation and recoveryMechanical response under experimental conditionsWhether the setup and skin tension matched between comparisons
An observer’s photo ratingHow a person assessed an imageWhether lighting, distance and expression were controlled

A real measurement comparison

In a study on forearm skin, researchers recorded capacitance and several mechanical parameters before and 60 minutes after five moisturizers were applied. They also investigated the role of pretension in how the skin responded. Before treating two “hydration” numbers as interchangeable, look at which number came from the electrical measurement and which came from the mechanical test.

Apply that to a wellness claim

Suppose an ad cites a short-term forearm reading to claim that a drink improves how your face looks over a month. The product, test site, time and outcome have changed. The study needed for the ad would have to test those conditions. The photo-ratings comparison shows why observer scores require their own interpretation.
