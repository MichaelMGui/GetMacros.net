An interim U.S. policy on certain compounded forms of BPC-157 is different from FDA approval of a drug. In 2026, HHS set out circumstances in which it does not intend to take enforcement action while rulemaking is underway. Appendix B discusses BPC-157 acetate. The document does not establish that BPC-157 improves recovery or that online products match what the policy describes.

Three documents that can appear in claims about BPC-157
Document or actionWhat it can establish
FDA staff reviewWhat evidence the scientific reviewers considered and the gaps they identified
Advisory committee voteThe committee’s recommendation on a compounding list
HHS interim enforcement policyHow the department says it intends to exercise enforcement discretion in specified circumstances during rulemaking

Why the documents can reach different conclusions

FDA’s July briefing review identified gaps in the ability to characterize the substance and in the evidence on safety and effectiveness. HHS’s later appendix says it considered additional material, including documentation of use and a lot-specific certificate. Those documents can inform a policy decision, but a test of what is in a particular lot answers a different question from a well-controlled trial of whether a treatment produces a stated benefit.

Read the verb in a headline

If a post says regulators “cleared” a peptide, look for which decision it links to. A recommendation, a policy about enforcement and a product-specific approval have distinct meanings. FDA explains that compounded drugs do not undergo the same premarket review of safety, effectiveness and quality as FDA-approved drugs.
