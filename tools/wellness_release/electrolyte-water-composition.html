Water provides fluid. A drink formulated with electrolytes also supplies dissolved ions such as sodium or potassium, in amounts that depend on the recipe. Plain water can contain minerals too. The useful comparison is the amount of each nutrient in the volume you drink.

Three things a drink description should let you check
Line to readWhat it tells you
Serving volumeHow much fluid the nutrient declaration describes.
Sodium and potassiumThe listed milligrams of those separate electrolytes for that serving.
Carbohydrate and sugarWhether the drink also supplies carbohydrate. A sugar line is not a measure of electrolytes.

Use the full serving context

On a U.S. sports-drink label such as Lemon Lime Gatorade, sodium, potassium and carbohydrate have their own figures. If you compare products with different volumes, scale them to an equal volume first and then calculate what you would actually drink. Tap-water minerals depend on the local supply, so use a local water quality report if you need that comparison.

The label describes the drink and does not establish what a specific workout requires. Work through sodium per bottle with exact serving sizes.
