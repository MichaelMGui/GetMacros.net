The word “peptide” describes a kind of molecule made from linked amino acids. On its own, it does not tell you which molecule, what the product is intended to do, or how the FDA regulates it. A collagen peptide powder sold as a supplement and a peptide drug intended to be injected are different products. A study of one cannot establish the safety or effectiveness of the other.

Catch a category swap in a claim

Imagine an ad says, “Peptides were studied for skin, so our product’s claim is proved.” Ask whether the study evaluated an ingested powder, a topical formulation or a drug. Then check the ingredient and outcome. Even two topical products can have different formulations. A headline that supplies only the word “peptides” leaves too much unknown to make the comparison.

Read regulatory language at the product level

Being sold in a shop, being compounded and being approved are different regulatory facts. FDA’s supplement and compounding guidance explains how to check those terms. For an unfamiliar product, look for the exact name, intended route and official sources. A broad label category cannot establish whether an individual product is appropriate to use.
