A claim about a named antioxidant nutrient describes something in the product. A claim about what happens to people after consuming the product needs a different kind of evidence. In the U.S., the FDA’s nutrient-content rules set conditions for antioxidant nutrient claims, including which nutrient is named and what is known about its activity.

Three claims with different evidence requirements
Claim typeWhat to check
Food provides an amount of vitamin CThe declared amount and serving size.
A specific biochemical activity is presentWhich property the source tested and how.
People had a better health outcomeThe relevant study population, comparator and actual measured outcome.

Start with the named nutrient

If a package names vitamin C, compare the amounts for matching servings. If a seller claims a concrete health effect, look for research on that specific claim rather than borrowing the label statement. An amount listed in milligrams and a difference in clinical events are measurements of different things.
