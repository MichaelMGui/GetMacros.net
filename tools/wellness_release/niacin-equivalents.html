Niacin equivalents account for both niacin already in food and a contribution from the amino acid tryptophan. The NIH reference defines one equivalent as 1 mg of niacin or 60 mg of tryptophan. Before comparing figures, check whether a table already combined the two.

Work through one food on both bases

The NIH table lists 10 mg of niacin already present in three ounces of roasted turkey breast meat. NIH separately gives about 180 mg tryptophan in a three-ounce turkey breast example. By the reference convention, that is about 3 mg in niacin equivalents. Combining these rounded examples illustrates about 13 mg NE. If a source already lists the total in equivalents, adding the tryptophan again would count it twice.

Use the same kind of figure on both sides

The 4.2 mg for peanuts is the amount of niacin present. Comparing it directly with a turkey estimate that includes tryptophan would mix bases. Compare 4.2 mg with 10 mg to answer the food-content question at those portions. For a full NE comparison, find a matching tryptophan value for both foods. Even then, the converted amount is a reference estimate. NIH notes that actual tryptophan conversion varies.

Niacin and folate use separate accounting conventions. The folate label guide explains when a similar-looking label number would double count folic acid.
