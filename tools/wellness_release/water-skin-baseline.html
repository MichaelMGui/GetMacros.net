Some water-intake studies report changes in instrument readings on skin. They give limited evidence that drinking extra water improves skin in someone who already drinks enough. To apply the finding, first check whether the study measured hydration status or simply grouped people by their usual intake.

Check where the result appeared

Forty-three participants completed the study. Researchers found no significant baseline difference in measured hydration of the outer skin or measured water loss between the higher and lower reported intake groups. The clear four-week increases highlighted in the higher-intake group were in the moisturizer and combined arms, on sites including the forearm, hand and shin. A result in the combined arm cannot establish a water-only effect.

A 2015 study also reported changes in skin readings, especially among people with lower prior intake. It used different group sizes and a dietary questionnaire to classify starting intake. In a headline about visible “glow,” check whether the researchers actually measured appearance. The skin measurement guide explains what the readings represent.
