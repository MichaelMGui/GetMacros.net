A gua sha trial measured changes in distances along the face after eight weeks. That does not establish a permanent change to the jawbone. The 2025 study compared two massage tools, and the measurements it collected answer a narrower question than a video promising a permanent new jawline.

What the trial could compare

Ahn and colleagues assigned 34 women aged 20 to 50 to a facial roller group or a gua sha group. The paper reports reductions of about 2.2–2.4 mm in the measured facial surface distances within the gua sha group. The roller group also had changes. These are distances across specified external landmarks, measured over the study period. They do not describe a measured change in the size or shape of bone.

Match the claim to the evidence it needs
ClaimWhat this study can say
“The face measured differently”The authors measured facial surface distances with 3D scanning before and after the intervention.
“The jawbone changed”That would require a measure of the bone itself. The reported measures were surface distances, muscle properties and skin elasticity.
“The result lasts after you stop”The authors call for longer follow-up. The study does not establish persistence after the intervention.
“The tool beats no intervention”The trial compared a roller with gua sha. It did not have a no-massage group.

How to read the next before-and-after claim

Ask what was measured, when the final measurement was taken, and what the comparison group did. A photo can show how a face looked at that moment. A controlled measurement and longer follow-up would answer different questions. If the claim says “permanent,” look for evidence that the outcome lasted after the intervention ended.
