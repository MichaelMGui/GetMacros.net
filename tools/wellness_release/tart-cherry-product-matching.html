A bottle labeled tart cherry juice is not automatically the beverage used in a marathon recovery trial. In the Howatson study, runners were assigned a specific cherry drink or a comparator. The protocol included days before the event as well as days after it. Changing either the beverage or the schedule changes the comparison.

To check whether a store product matches the trial
Match pointWhat to look for
FormulationWas the study beverage a blend, straight juice, concentrate or a reconstituted concentrate? Read both ingredient descriptions.
Concentration and servingCheck the prepared volume and any reported composition. A small bottle of concentrate and a ready-to-drink glass can use different denominators.
Event and timingFind the measured event and collection times. A post-workout drink is a different schedule from an intervention that starts before a race.

Use a missing composition value honestly

If a grocery label gives total sugar but no measured anthocyanin amount, matching its sugar content to another beverage cannot establish that the two drinks have equivalent anthocyanins. Keep the grocery label usable for logging, and keep the research comparison open until the missing information is available. Read why the trial’s strength and soreness outcomes differ.
