A “clinical grade” phrase on a U.S. skincare package does not, by itself, identify a trial or show that FDA approved a benefit. Start with the result the product is claiming. FDA explains that ordinary cosmetic labeling generally does not undergo premarket approval, and intended use can change how a product is regulated.

Separate a prominent phrase from the supporting information
Example label wordingWhat would make the claim more informative?
“Clinical grade”A precise explanation of what was tested and what claim the manufacturer is making
“Tested by a dermatologist”The method, purpose of the test and results
“Improved the appearance of dryness after two weeks”How appearance was measured, whether there was a comparison group, and whether this exact formula was tested

Look up the outcome, not just the study name

Take a fictional cream that says it was tested and shows a number on the box. A self-reported softness score from people who used it for a week answers a different question from an instrument measurement of moisture. If only one ingredient was studied, check whether the finished cream and its conditions of use were tested separately.

When the claim changes category

FDA distinguishes claims about appearance from claims to treat or prevent disease or affect the body’s structure or function. A cream’s front label might sound cosmetic while another part of its marketing makes a drug claim. The words “clinical grade” alone do not settle that question.
