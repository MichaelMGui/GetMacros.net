The cortisol awakening response describes how cortisol changes shortly after waking. In research, a measurement taken at 8 a.m. means something different for someone who just woke up and someone who has been awake for an hour. The elapsed time is part of what the researchers need to know.

All three observations share a clock time. They represent different points after waking. A comparison of the first and second row cannot tell you which participant had a larger rise, because each row records only one time point. It would take measurements at known points within each participant’s timeline to compare a change.

Look for how the study checked the timing

The expert consensus guidance describes a marked rise in cortisol in the first 30 to 45 minutes after awakening and emphasizes timing accuracy in research. The update revisits how studies check when people actually wake and take samples. A chart labeled “morning cortisol” might show a single morning level. Check the methods before treating that chart as a measurement of the awakening response.

Here is a way to check a headline about an intervention: identify whether it reports a difference at one time point, a change from waking, or a different profile across the day.
